# First product-workflow examples

Source: Anik's answers in the skill-library setup conversation on 2026-09-17. These are normalized notes from his descriptions, not independently inspected case studies. No source code, reference site contents, screenshots, client brief, or delivery results were provided.

## Taveco Air

Anik described taking inspiration from Tripnest and building around the client's brand, marketing or vibe, aesthetic design, packages, other content, and section flows.

Supported observation: a reference can inform structure and flows while the implementation is adapted to the client. This is evidence for a reference-inspired adaptation path, not for exact cloning or a fixed similarity percentage.

Still unknown: which reference elements were selected, the sequence of research and design, the MVP boundary, validation, and what Anik liked or disliked about the result.

## Mollah Auto

Anik said the client described issues, and he thought through and built Mollah Auto.

Supported observation: client problems can be the starting input for a solution; a supplied visual reference is not always the starting point.

Still unknown: the actual issues, users, domain requirements, how he chose among solutions, scope, and the outcome. Do not infer inventory, repair, sales, or other specific features from the name.

## Home-service project

Anik described a client supplying a reference and asking for approximately 80% similarity while changing colors, accents, and vibe.

Supported observation: the client's requested level and dimensions of similarity can be a project constraint. Branding changes may be requested while other reference elements remain close.

Still unknown: which dimensions were meant to match, what approval criteria were used, what features were implemented, and the result. Do not apply 80% to other projects or claim a measured score.

## Safe generalization

Choose a discovery path based on the actual client input: reference inspiration, stated problems, requested similarity, or a combination. Keep the client's context central. Ask for the missing fact when it changes a decision.

These examples do not by themselves establish Anik's preferred task order, MVP readiness rule, favored architecture, or universal design philosophy. The reusable maps in the [product method](product-method.md) are proposed tools for expressing the observed patterns.

## Subsequent project-framing clarification

On 2026-09-17, Anik said he maps the core user journey, considers whether the customer context is technical or nontechnical, and identifies whether the project is a solution, portfolio, business portfolio, corporate website, ERP, ecommerce, logistics, or another domain.

Supported preference: establish the core journey, audience context, kind of product, and business domain when framing a product. The classification distinguishes product purpose from industry and remains open to other categories.

Proposed interpretation for implementation: distinguish the client or buyer from actual end users and operators when their technical familiarity differs; use a compact framing brief. These details are useful ways to apply the stated considerations, not a claimed verbatim method.

The answer arrived under a readiness question but describes project framing. It does not confirm what makes a first version ready, a rigid order for every task, or a particular prioritization framework.
